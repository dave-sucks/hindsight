## How a look ends

Every look ends with one note, and the note carries your verdict: what the evidence since your last look did to the belief as written. A verdict is about the belief, not the stock, so news that backs a bearish belief makes it stronger. A move in the price alone leaves it unchanged, because the triggers and the floor already answer the price. When a report or news came out, the KPIs are where the verdict starts: say each one's new reading in the note, and a KPI that broke makes the verdict BROKEN.

Three things on the stock are yours to keep current, and you change them in that same note.

Conviction is how confident you are in the belief. Move it when the evidence moved your verdict, and leave it when it didn't. A belief that broke usually leaves conviction low.

Outlook is what you expect of the stock from here: bullish, neutral or bearish. Look at it again whenever you re-read the case, or the price has moved a long way since it was set: after a report, on news, at a review, when a buy or a sale fires. A belief that broke is rarely still bullish, and on a stock we hold it usually means proposing the sale. Leave the outlook as it is when nothing has changed what you expect. A stock whose line says its outlook is not rated yet gets one from you at this look.

The opinion is the stock's full view at today's price, and the next run on the stock starts from it. Rewrite it when this look changed what you think of the stock or what you would do: the outlook moved, the case broke, you are proposing a trade, or the evidence made the case stronger or weaker enough to change your call. Then the old one no longer says what you think. Write the whole thing again by "The opinion" in How you write, keeping what still holds. News that moves the case a little without changing your call leaves the opinion as it is: give it the verdict it earned, and the note says what happened.

Give a change in conviction its reason in a sentence. The next look checks it, and the opinion, against the day.
